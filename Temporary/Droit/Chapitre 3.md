# 1. presentation du cadre general du commerce electronique
## Definitions 

**Two Ways to Look at It:** Because of this, we have to look at two different types of definitions: a **narrow** one (which limits it) and a **broad** one (which includes a lot).

### 1. The Narrow (Restrictive) Definition

- **It’s ONLY the Sale:** The narrow definition says that e-commerce is **only the act of selling something online.** It strictly focuses on the transaction itself.
- **Who is Involved:** It involves two parties trying to complete a sale (buyer and seller). It often implies that the seller is a **professional business.**
- **What it DOES NOT Include:** 
    - **Delivery/Shipping** (logistics/transport).
    - **Data Processing** (handling the information).
    - **All other general internet services.**

**The Legal View:** A Tunisian law says that **"electronic commerce"** simply means **"commercial deals done through electronic messages."** And an **"electronic message"** is just a **"digital document."**. e-commerce is essentially a **"commercial operation"** that is carried out using a **"digital document."**

### 2- The Broad (Extensive) Definition of Electronic Commerce

This view is much wider and more inclusive:

- **It’s More Than Just the Sale:** This definition includes the simple online sale, but **it also adds all the other services** provided through the Internet—whether those services are free or paid for.
- **The Scope:** Simply put, if any business activity uses **electronic communication networks** (like the internet) to operate, it falls under this broad definition of e-commerce.

## 2. The Areas E-commerce Covers (Scope of Application)

Even though the original Tunisian law wasn't specific about where e-commerce rules apply, legal experts generally categorize online business into different areas based on who is doing the buying and selling

- **Business to Administration (B to A):**
    - This is an online deal between a **Business (company)** and a **Government/Administration** (like a tax office).
- **Business to Business (B to B):**
    - This is the **largest and most important** area of e-commerce.
    - It involves an online operation between **two or more professional businesses**. (For instance, a car manufacturer ordering parts from a supplier online).
- **Business to Consumer (B to C):**
    - This is the type of e-commerce **most people are familiar with**.
    - It is an online operation between a **Business** and a **private person/shopper (a consumer)**.
- **Consumer to Consumer (C to C):**
	- This is a **new model** that has appeared in recent years.
	- It is an online operation involving **only consumers themselves**.

## 3. Characteristics of e-commerce

e-commerce is a **commercial activity done at a distance using electronic means**. It identifies two main characteristics:

**Activity Done at a Distance:**

- This means the activity happens through electronic communication methods.
- This feature is **not unique** to e-commerce, as other types of distance selling exist (e.g., mail order, telephone sales).
- The law defines **distance selling** as using a means of communication with the consumer that allows the merchant to sell a product or service _outside_ of their usual sales premises. Means used can include telephone, video, radio, postal service, or catalogs.

**Activity Done by Electronic Means:**

- This is the **main, distinguishing feature** of e-commerce.
- It means using **binary technology** (computer technology) to achieve the commercial operation.
- The Internet is **not the only way** to do e-commerce, but it currently dominates because it is easy to access and interactive.

## 4. Legal framework of e-commerce

selling things remotely requires many different legal rules, so countries have to adjust their national laws to match international standards.

#### **In Tunisia (National Level):**

E-commerce is mainly regulated by **several specific laws**:

- **Law n°83 of 9/8/2000**: The core law related to exchanges and e-commerce.
- **Law n° 2005-51 of June 27, 2005**: This law deals with the electronic transfer of funds.
- **Law n° 117 of December 7, 1992**: This law is about consumer protection.
- **Law n°40 of June 2, 1998**: This law covers technical rules for sales and commercial advertising.

#### **International Level:**

At the international level, e-commerce is regulated by things like the **UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce (LTEC)**.

- This model law (adopted in 1996) gives national governments a set of rules that are accepted worldwide.
- **Goal:** The purpose is to remove legal barriers and make this type of commerce more secure and trustworthy.

Beyond official laws written by the government, e-commerce can also be governed by other types of rules:

- **Codes of Conduct:** * The first effort to regulate e-commerce was done in 1987 by the **International Chamber of Commerce (ICC)**.
    
    - The ICC created the **UNCID (Uniform Rules of Conduct for Interchange of Trade Data by Teletransmission)**, which are general rules for exchanging data electronically.
        
- **Contractual Practices:**
    
    - These are based on the **agreement (consent)** given by the parties who are making the contract (buyers and sellers) involved in the e-commerce transaction..
    - The way contracts are handled, along with self-regulation rules, is very important. They help create a kind of order that arises on the market by itself, rather than being imposed by national laws.
        
- **Labels (Certification Marks):**
    
    - There are two types of labeling:
        1. **Internal Labeling:** This is when the website's owners promise to follow certain rules. This commitment may lead to a code of conduct or a trust charter.
        2. **External Labeling**: This is when a commitment is developed and monitored by **third parties** (independent organizations).These third-party organizations **regularly check (audit = inspection)** the website to make sure it is following the rules it promised to uphold.
	        -  **Getting the Label:**
				1. The website must first **meet a number of conditions** set by the third-party organization.
				2. Once the conditions are met, the website is **subject to regular audits**.
				3. The results of the check are put into a **report**.
				4. If the report is **positive**, the website is given an **official certification seal** (a logo or badge) that it can display. This seal is meant to show customers that the site is trustworthy and compliant.

# 2. presentation and general framework from e-commerce

## **The Electronic Contract**

- **What it is:** An electronic contract is a **virtual, unchangeable** agreement. It is not limited by geography, meaning it can be made anywhere in the world.

- **Connection to Paper Contracts:** According to **Article 1 of the Law of August 9, 2000**. This law says that the standard rules for **written (paper) contracts** generally **apply** to electronic contracts. This applies to things like:

	- **Agreement (Expression of consent):** How both parties show they agree to the terms.
	- **Legal Force (Legal effect):** What the contract is actually allowed to do under the law.
	- **Being Legal (Validity):** Whether the contract is properly formed and legally sound.
	- **Carrying it Out (Execution):** How the obligations of the contract are fulfilled.

### 1. Classification of electronic contract

- **Definition:** An electronic contract is defined as a contract made **at a distance** using **information and communication technologies** (like the internet).
- **Not a New Contract Type:** Despite all its specific characteristics (being virtual, done at a distance), the electronic contract **does not create a new category of contract** (meaning a contract for hosting a website is considered the same as contract for renting).

### 2. Consensual Contract vs. Contract of Adhesion

#### **The Ideal (Consensual Contract)**

- **What it means:** Legally, a contract should be "consensual." This means it is born from the **mutual agreement** and **free will** of both parties (the buyer and the seller).
- **How it works online:** The theory says an electronic contract is formed when the parties show they agree remotely using digital tools.

#### **The Reality (Contract of Adhesion)**

- **The problem:** In many online contracts, especially for consumers, the mutual agreement part is not entirely true.
- **What it is:** Most online agreements are **Contracts of Adhesion** (or "take-it-or-leave-it" contracts).
- **Why:** The business (seller) writes all the terms and rules (clauses), and the customer **cannot negotiate** or change any of them. The customer can only click "I Agree" or walk away.

**The Principle Stays:** Despite the consumer having no power to negotiate, the law still considers the contract to be legally formed and valid because the principle of **consensualism** is still met.

### 3. Internal Contract vs. International Contract

- **The Problem:** One of the difficulties with electronic contracts is determining their **attachment** (the legal jurisdiction). Without a physical territory (a _territorial basis_), it is hard to decide if a contract is **internal** (domestic) or **international**.
- **The Issues:** This lack of territory creates serious problems concerning which country's **law should apply**, which **court is competent**, and so on.
- **A Partial Solution:** If the contract involves something physical that happens in the real world (lke **delivering merchandise**), the problem is _partially_ solved. The physical location where the goods are delivered gives the contract a clear connection to a specific country's territory.
- **Hard Cases (Totally Online):** If the contract is **100% digital** (concluded and executed entirely online, like buying a software license or a service), lawyers have to use **economic or functional criteria**. This means they look at things like:
	- Where the business makes its money.
	- Where the service is mainly used.
	- Whether the contract involves a significant exchange of value across countries.

### 4. The Conditions of Validity of Electronic Contracts

- **A**: **Article 3 of the Tunisian Code of Obligations and Contracts (COC)**, which establishes the general rule for capacity:

> "**Every person is capable of obligating others and themselves unless they are not declared incapable by law.**"

**What this means:** In simple terms, the law assumes every person is legally allowed to enter into a contract unless a judge or the law has explicitly stated that they cannot (for instance, minors)

**Legal Distinctions:** The law distinguishes between three groups of people when it comes to contracting:

1. Those who have **full capacity** (full legal right to contract).
2. Those who have **limited capacity** (can contract only under certain conditions or with assistance).
3. Those who have **no capacity** (cannot legally enter into a contract at all).

- **b. Valid declaration of will:** This refers to having true and genuine consent, meaning the agreement isn't flawed by **vices of consent** like **Violence**, **Error** or **Fraud**.
    
- **c. A certain object:** The contract must be about definite things, facts, or intangible rights that are allowed (legal) in commerce. such as
	-  **Things** (e.g., a physical product being sold).
    - **Facts** (e.g., a service being performed, like hosting a website).
    - **Intangible Rights** (e.g., intellectual property or the right to use software).
    
- **d. A lawful cause:** Every contract must have a legal and valid "cause" (reason). A contract that has **no cause** or is based on an **unlawful (illicite) cause** is automatically not applicable. A cause is unlawful if the reason for the contract is against:

    - **Good Morals (bonnes mœurs):** What society considers ethical and decent behavior.
    - **Public Order**: Rules that are essential for the fundamental structure and stability of society.
    - **The Law:** Directly violating an existing statute.

### 5. Offer and Acceptance in the Electronic Contract

Traditional contract law was designed for face-to-face deals. The electronic sales contract is different because of:

- **Distance:** The parties are far apart when the contract is made.
- **Identification:** It is difficult to physically identify the other party.
- **Digital Format:** The contract document is unchangeable.

 **Legal Framework**

- After the **Law of August 9, 2000**, was adopted, the Tunisian government created specific rules for e-commerce.
- **Goals:** These rules aim to protect consumers, defend commercial freedom on the Internet, and generally ensure freedom of communication.
- **The Offer:** This is the clear or silent way a person proposes a contract to others (specific people or the public) under certain conditions.
- **The Acceptance:** This is the clear or silent way a person accepts and confirms the offer that was made to them.

#### A.  The Electronic Offer

The **Law of August 9, 2000**, requires the seller to have an **obligation to inform** the consumer. This information must cover the period **before** the contract is finalized (pre-contractual phase) and also the rules for **how the contract will be carried out** (execution).

**Required Information (The Four Categories)**

The electronic offer must include specific details that can be sorted into four categories, according to Article 25 of the law:

1. **Seller's Identity:** Who you are buying from, including their name, address, telephone number, etc..
2. **Transaction Subject:** What you are buying, including the nature, main features, and the price of the product or service.
3. **Contractual Conditions:** The details of the deal, such as delivery methods, the delivery deadline, confirmation conditions, fees, costs, charges, and payment procedures.
4. **Dispute Resolution:** Information on how legal problems or disagreements will be handled after the transaction is finalized.

To ensure the consumer is well-informed, the law requires this information to be provided **electronically** so the consumer can look it up during **all stages** of the operation. The electronic offer must meet specific standards: it must be **firm, clear and precise**.

##### public and private offers

**Public Offer**

- **Target:** The offer can be addressed **to the public** (e.g., on a website) or emails to multiple individuals.
- **Seller's Choice:** In the first case (public offer), the merchant **cannot choose their buyers**.
- **Seller's Protections:** However, the seller is allowed to insert clauses into the offer that protect themselves. These conditions include:
    
    - **Reserving the offer** to a specific geographical area.
    - **Limiting the offer** based on available stock (stock limits).
        
- **Legal Risk:** If the seller cannot fulfill the demand, they risk being sued for misleading advertising . In this case, the valid offer follows the rule of **"first come, first served"** .

 **Personalized Offer (_Offre nominative_)**

- **Target:** This is when the offer is intended for a **specific, named person** (e.g., by email).
- **Seller is Bound:** When the email is nominative (named), the offeror (seller) is **bound by their offer** to the specific recipient.
- **General Mail Rule:** If the email is **not nominative** (not addressed to a specific person), the offer is considered public, and the seller is only bound to the **first person who accepts** it.

##### Duration of the offer

- **Law's Requirement:** Given the specific nature of online sales and the need to protect consumers, **Article 25** of the Law of August 9, 2000, requires the seller to inform the consumer **before the conclusion of the contract** about the **duration of the offer at the fixed prices**.
- **Seller is Committed:** Anytime an offer includes a specific **deadline for acceptance**, the seller is **committed** to the other party until that deadline expires.
- **Seller is Released:** Conversely, the seller is **released** from the commitment if **no response** reaches them within the fixed deadline.

### Electronic acceptance
#### **1. The Act of Acceptance**

- **Manifesting Will:** The consumer must clearly show their explicit intent to contract. 
- **The Online Form:** Acceptance online is usually done by **clicking the acceptance button** on the web page.
- **Confirmation is Key:** For the acceptance to be valid, the online buyer must then **confirm their acceptance**. This is crucial because a simple, mistaken move of the mouse should not bind a user who had no intention to accept the offer.

#### **2. The Legal Requirements for Acceptance (Article 27)**

To protect the consumer, **Article 27** of the Law of 2000 requires a specific procedure _before_ the contract is finalized:

- The seller must allow the consumer to **definitively recap** (review) all their choices.
- The consumer must have the option to **confirm the order** or **modify it** according to their will (correcting errors).

#### **3. When and Where the Contract is Formed (Article 28)**

- **Default Rule:** Unless the parties agree otherwise, the contract is concluded at the **seller's address** and on the **date the seller accepts the order**.
- **Seller's Duty to Confirm:** The contract is confirmed by the seller sending a **signed electronic document** to the consumer.

#### The Right of Withdrawal and Restitution for the Consumer

##### 1. The Right of Withdrawal

The law gives the consumer the ability to cancel the contract even after it is formed.
	
- **Seller's Duty to Inform:** **Article 25** requires the seller to specify the right of withdrawal, including the deadline.
- **The Default Deadline (Article 30):** If the contract doesn't specify a rule, the consumer can always exercise their right of withdrawal for **10 days**.
- **Calculating the Deadline:** The starting date for the 10 days depends on the item:
    - For **merchandise (goods):** The delay starts from the **date of reception**.
    - For **services:** The delay starts from the **date of the conclusion of the contract**.
- **Consequence of Withdrawal:** The seller must return the amount paid by the consumer (plus related expenses) within **10 working days** from the date the merchandise is returned or the service is canceled. The consumer is responsible for the **cost of returning the goods**.

##### 2. returning for Product Defects

The consumer also has the possibility of **restitution** (return) for product defects. **Article 31** provides this right in two cases:

- **difference** of the product to the order.
- Failure to respect the **delivery deadlines** for the merchandise.

#####  3. Cases Where Withdrawal is Impossible (Article 32)

There are cases where the consumer loses the right to withdraw from the contract:

- If the consumer requests delivery **before the standard delivery date**.
- For **personalized products** or products that cannot be reshipped, or products that might damaged or expired (e.g., food).
- When the consumer accesses **recordings or software** (in case of copying the digital content).
- **Purchases of magazines or newspapers**.

> **Important Note:** These exceptions are **not applicable** if there are obvious or hidden defects in the purchased products.
